## 1. Social space ↔ Social structure (constitutive dependence)

| Bourdieu passage | Ritchie term | Link |
|---|---|---|
| "a space of differences based on a principle of economic and social differentiation" (p. 3) | **Social structure** — "a structure that is constitutively dependent on social factors" (Ritchie p. 407) | The social space is Bourdieu's structure-of-relations; its nodes (positions) are defined relationally, matching Ritchie's node/edge formalism (p. 405). |
| "[t]he real is the relational; reality is nothing other than the structure, as a set of constant relationships..." (p. 3) | **Structure** (general) — nodes/edges/system (p. 405) | Bourdieu's "relational realism" is a sociological gloss on Ritchie's structuralist thesis that groups are structure-involving (p. 420). |
| Social space built from economic, cultural, social, and symbolic capital (pp. 4–5) | **Nodes defined by relations + occupancy restrictions** (p. 405) | Capital volume/composition/trajectory fix an agent's "node" (position); the dimensions are the structure's defining relations. |

## 2. "Classes on paper" / "theoretical classes" ↔ Feature social groups (doubly covert, per manuscript)

| Bourdieu passage | Ritchie term | Link |
|---|---|---|
| "constructed classes... sets of agents who, by virtue of occupying similar positions in social space... are endowed with similar dispositions" (p. 6) | **Feature social group** — node in an *external* structure, defined by shared features (pp. 414–417) | Theoretical classes are exactly Ritchie's feature groups: a taxonomic node unified by feature-sharing (here, position-conditioned dispositions), not by functional organization. |
| "a theoretical class...might be considered as a probable real class...whose constituents are likely to be brought together...but are not actually mobilized" (p. 7) | **Feature group vs. organized group distinction**; non-identity of structural position and group-membership-by-occupying-nodes-of-an-internal-structure (p. 412) | Marks the exact boundary Ritchie draws between a node in an external structure (feature group) and a structured whole (organized group). |
| "meet all the requirements of a scientific taxonomy, at once predictive and descriptive" (p. 6) | **Social kind** (Koslicki's definition, adopted by Ritchie p. 415–416) — "categories... into which objects may be grouped on the basis of shared characteristics" | Manuscript explicitly treats doubly covert groups as Boyd-style property-cluster kinds; Ritchie's "social kind" is the superordinate category feature groups fall under. |
| Danger that "the more accurate the theoretical construction...the greater the chance that they will be seen as real groups" (pp. 4–5) | **Goldilocks Constraint** (p. 403) — distinguishing genuine groups from over-generated ones | Both authors worry about the same overgeneration/reification risk, from opposite directions: Ritchie wants to not *exclude* real feature groups; Bourdieu warns against *promoting* a feature group to organized-group status without warrant. |

## 3. "Real"/"practical"/"mobilized" classes ↔ Organized social groups

| Bourdieu passage | Ritchie term | Link |
|---|---|---|

## 5. Groupmaking, naming, delegation ↔ Intentional creation/instantiation (performative institution) — ✅ revision 5 implemented

| Bourdieu passage | Ritchie term | Link |
|---|---|---|
| "the movement from probability to reality, from the theoretical class to practical class, is never given" (p. 7) | **Non-automatic transition** from feature group to organized group; grounding is not identity | Directly licenses the manuscript's "dispositional"/probabilistic grounding relation: feature-group membership only raises the objective chance of organized-group formation. |
| "assenting to a definite form of existence...at the cost of a specific work" — production of a representation of divisions (p. 8) | **Intentionally created group** — performative act of institution collectively accepted by members (Ritchie's "standard story," via Searle, as glossed in manuscript) | Representation-work is the performative act; recognition by the represented is the "collective acceptance" condition. |
| "the performative act of naming...brings into existence in an instituted form...what hitherto existed only as a serial collection" (p. 14) | **Intentional creation**, origin-story criterion (Ritchie p. 408–409) | This is Bourdieu's clearest statement of a Searlean/Ritchean performative-institution event converting a mere feature-sharing collection into an organized group. |

| Bourdieu passage | Ritchie term | Link |

## 8. Resolving the substantialism/covert-structure overlap with Mallon's reification framework — ✅ footnotes 6–7 revised; ✅ revision 4 (trichotomy surfaced in main text) implemented

Ritchie's overt/covert distinction (cited by the manuscript via "Mallon's (2016) sense" of reification) is a borrowed label; Mallon's own chapter, "Social Roles and Reification" (2016, pp. 431–445), supplies the actual mechanism that §4 above only gestures at. Pulling it in directly — rather than at Ritchie's second hand — sharpens two things the manuscript currently leaves blurred: (a) what exactly "substantialism" consists in, and (b) why the manuscript's "doubly covert" feature groups are *not* automatically covert in Mallon's technical sense.

### 8.1 Substantialism *is* reification, precisely stated

| Bourdieu passage | Mallon term | Link |
|---|---|---|
| "[o]ne has to avoid turning into necessary and intrinsic properties of some group...the properties which rest with this group at a given moment because of its position in a definite social space" (1989/1991, p. 630) | **Reification**: "the process by which facts produced by social arrangements later come to be conceived as existing and having come about independently of those relations" (Mallon, p. 434) | This is a near-exact match. Bourdieu's "substantialism" names the same operation Mallon formalizes: a relational, position-dependent fact (capital-conditioned disposition) is mistaken for an intrinsic, position-independent one. |
| Position-dependent properties mistaken for essential ones generates what Bourdieu elsewhere calls the "misrecognition" of social hierarchy as natural order | **Folk essentialism + fundamental attribution error** as the specific cognitive mechanisms stabilizing covert constructions (Mallon, p. 436): behavior "explained by appeal to intrinsic features of individuals rather than features of their situation" | Mallon supplies the manuscript's missing cognitive-mechanism vocabulary. Footnote 6 of the manuscript simply asserts that Bourdieu "did not deny the existence of intrinsic, socially derived properties" without saying *how* an agent comes to misattribute a relational property as intrinsic; Mallon's answer is a specific, independently motivated psychological bias, not just a sociological placeholder. |
| Haslanger's gender-as-relational-property argument, which Bourdieu's own relational-realist framework structurally parallels ("gender is a relational or extrinsic property of individuals, and the relations in question are social," quoted by Mallon p. 433, from Haslanger 2012) | **Landlord/gender analogy**: role occupancy is relational/systemic, not a matter of sharing an intrinsic feature (Mallon, p. 433) | Confirms that Bourdieu's and Mallon's starting diagnosis of the error is identical — both treat "class" and "gender" properties as constituted by position in a system of relations, and both see substantialism/reification as the error of detaching the property from that system. |

### 8.2 Why the manuscript's "doubly covert" groups are not simply Mallon-covert

Mallon's covert constructions are not merely *unrecognized*; they are **specifically naturalized** — stabilized by a prior act of collective forgetting in which a category once known to be an institutional artifact is later taken, falsely, to be natural (Mallon, pp. 434–436, 439). This matters for precision:

- The manuscript's "doubly covert feature groups" (statistically discovered clusters of capital-holders with no folk label at all, e.g., Bourdieu's theoretical classes as property-cluster kinds) have never been collectively represented *at all*, let alone misrepresented as natural. They are simply unrecognized, not (yet) reified. Mallon's apparatus does not straightforwardly apply to them, because there is no prior "We intend that X's count as Y's" episode to be forgotten (Mallon's formal schema, p. 442).
- Mallon's covert constructions apply more precisely to the manuscript's *singly* covert, explicitly recognized "social kind" groups (race, gender, sexuality, class *as popularly believed to be natural*) — categories the folk do recognize as real but (mistakenly) believe to be natural rather than socially produced. This is the case Mallon actually analyzes via race, gender, and the Plato "noble lie" examples (pp. 431–432, 441).
- Substantialism, in Bourdieu, operates at exactly this second level: once a theoretical/feature-group partition becomes folk-recognized (a "social kind"), the risk is that its members' position-conditioned properties get re-described as essential/natural features of the kind rather than as effects of position — i.e., Bourdieu's substantialism presupposes that Mallon's folk-level naturalization has already happened or is actively happening. The manuscript should therefore restrict the Mallon-style overt/covert axis to *recognized* feature groups undergoing (mis)naturalization, and keep the never-recognized "doubly covert" groups analytically separate, as a distinct (pre-reification) category with no Mallon analogue.

### 8.3 Consequence for the mapping

- **Bourdieu's substantialism = Mallon's reification**, applied to Ritchie's feature social groups once they are folk-recognized as "social kinds": a feature group's defining relation (position in social space) is forgotten/misrecognized, and its node-defining property is re-described as intrinsic.
- **Ritchie's covert social structure**, properly read through Mallon rather than merely cited to him, should be restricted to structures whose social-factor-dependence *was or could be* "openly acknowledged" but is currently misrecognized as natural — not to Bourdieu's never-yet-discovered doubly covert groups, which are a different, non-Mallon-covert category (structurally real but simply unrepresented, per Bourdieu 1987, p. 3 and p. 6).
- This also sharpens the manuscript's footnote 7 (Ritchie's 2018 "social creation" vs. "social construction" distinction): Mallon's reification is best read as a *third* process alongside those two — neither the creation of an organized group nor the ordinary construction of a feature group's properties, but the subsequent *misdescription* of an already-constructed feature group's properties as unconstructed. Keeping these three processes terminologically separate (creation / construction / reification) would resolve the overlap the manuscript currently compresses into the single word "substantialism."

## 9. Bourdieu's 1985 essay confirms the overt/covert axis is independent of the feature/organized axis — ✅ revisions 2 and 3 implemented

Bourdieu, "The Social Space and the Genesis of Groups" (*Social Science Information* 24(2), 1985, pp. 195–220) predates the 1987 lecture by two years and turns out to carry Bourdieu's *own* vocabulary for the folk-awareness (overt/covert) axis — independent of, and conceptually prior to, the theoretical-class/mobilized-group (feature/organized) axis worked out in 1987. This is useful confirmation, not just elaboration: it shows the two distinctions were analytically separate for Bourdieu from early on, rather than being an artifact of the manuscript's own Ritchie-based reconstruction.

### 9.1 Direct textual anchors for the overt/covert axis, independent of Mallon

| Bourdieu passage (1985) | Ritchie/Mallon term | Link |
|---|---|---|
| Symbolic capital is capital "when it is known and recognized as self-evident"; differences become "legitimate differences, which most often means differences in nature ('natural distinction')" (p. 204) | **Covert social structure** (Ritchie p. 408) / **reification** (Mallon p. 434) | This is a *better* primary citation than the 1989/1991 passage used in §8.1 — Bourdieu's own phrase "natural distinction" is a direct French-sociological counterpart to Mallon's "mistaken belief in naturalness." It should probably replace or supplement the 1989/1991 citation in the manuscript's revised footnote 6. |
| "the work of naturalization which every group tends to produce in order to legitimate itself" — the analyst must "reconstruct... the historical labour of which the divisions...are the product" (p. 214) | **Reification** as an active, group-interested process, not mere cognitive error (Mallon pp. 434, 442–443 on power/ideology alongside folk essentialism) | Bourdieu names the *mechanism* Mallon calls reification using his own term, "travail de naturalisation" ("work of naturalization"), and — like Mallon's discussion of "powerful interests" (p. 436) — treats it as interest-driven, not merely a passive cognitive slip. This strengthens §8's claim that substantialism = reification by showing Bourdieu has independent, non-Mallon vocabulary for exactly this process. |
| "Every field is the site of...struggle over the definition of the legitimate principles of division... the question of legitimacy arises from the very possibility of...a break with the doxa which takes the ordinary order for granted" (p. 207) | **Overt social structure** — dependence "openly acknowledged" upon reflection (Ritchie p. 408) | "Breaking with doxa" is literally the overt pole of Ritchie's axis: a structure moves from covert (doxic, taken for granted) to overt (acknowledged as dependent on social factors) precisely through the classification struggles Bourdieu describes. |

| "The capacity to make entities exist in the explicit state, to publish...that which had...remained in the state of individual or serial existence" (p. 202) | **Overt social structure** / performative institution (Ritchie's intentional-creation criterion, p. 408–409) | Note the overlap risk this passage creates: "serial existence" becoming "explicit" sounds like *both* axes at once — moving from covert to overt *and* from feature group to organized group. See §9.2. |

### 9.2 A genuine point of overlap the 1985 essay exposes (not fully resolved)

The INSEE occupational-title material (pp. 205–208) — agents contesting official classifications, e.g., an *instituteur* relabeling himself *enseignant* to "escape the verdict of the official taxonomy" (p. 206), and Bourdieu's remark that "one cannot conduct a science of classifications without conducting a science of the struggle over classifications" (p. 208) — is a case where **both** axes move together: an occupational category goes from unrecognized/informally-named (covert, and also a mere feature group) to officially sanctioned, legally recognized, state-classified (overt, and also closer to an organized-group designation via INSEE's institutional apparatus). Likewise, the "serial existence → explicit, public existence" passage (p. 202) and the delegation/spokesperson material already catalogued in §5 both describe a single historical event that simultaneously de-naturalizes a category *and* organizes it.

This suggests the two axes, while analytically separable (contra any assumption that overt = organized and covert = feature, which §4 and §8 above already warn against), are **empirically correlated** in Bourdieu's own examples: the political/symbolic work of naming and institutionalizing a group (feature → organized, via Ritchie's intentional-creation criterion) typically *also* performs the work of making the group's social-factor-dependence explicit (covert → overt). Bourdieu does not have a clean account of why these two transitions usually co-occur rather than, say, a group becoming "overt" (openly acknowledged as socially produced) without ever becoming "organized" (institutionally instantiated) — that remains an open question for the manuscript to address, not one the 1985 essay itself resolves.

### 9.3 Net assessment

The 1985 essay sharpens the overt/covert axis considerably by supplying Bourdieu's own terms — "natural distinction," "work of naturalization," "class unconscious," "break with the doxa," "serial" vs. "explicit" existence — in place of relying only on the 1989/1991 fragment and Mallon's secondhand vocabulary. It confirms the axis is genuinely distinct from the feature/organized (theoretical/practical) axis (note 6, p. 219, explicitly separates "sense of realities" from "class consciousness" as an *explicit representation*, which is a different question from whether the class is organized). But it also shows, via the INSEE and naming examples, that in Bourdieu's actual case studies the two transitions (covert→overt and feature→organized) tend to run together, which is a limitation worth flagging explicitly in the manuscript rather than leaving implicit.
